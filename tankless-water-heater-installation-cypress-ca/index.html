Installing a Tankless Hot Water Heater: What Cypress, CA Homes Need to Know

By the Cypress 24/7 Plumbing team. Published November 12, 2026.

A tankless water heater heats water only when you open a tap, so it never runs out and takes up far less space than a tank. Installing one is a bigger job than swapping a tank, because it needs the right gas supply, venting, a drain for condensate on some models and a permit.

This guide walks through the planning steps for a Cypress home, what to ask before you buy and how local conditions affect the choice.

How a tankless heater works

When you open a hot tap, a flow sensor tells the unit to fire a burner or energize an element. Water passes through a heat exchanger and leaves hot. When you close the tap, the unit shuts off. There is no stored water waiting to cool, which is the source of the efficiency claim.

The tradeoff is that the unit has to deliver the whole temperature rise in seconds. That demands more gas or more electrical capacity than a tank does at any given moment.

Step by step: what installation involves

First, a technician sizes the unit by adding the flow of the fixtures you expect to run together and by considering how cold the incoming water gets. Second, the gas line is checked.

Many homes have a gas pipe sized for a tank, and a tankless burner may need a larger line. Third, the vent path is planned, either through a wall or the roof, with the correct material for the unit.

Fourth, water connections are installed with isolation valves, which make future flushing easy. Fifth, condensing models need a drain for acidic condensate, and some need a neutralizer. Last, the unit is tested, the display is set and the old tank is removed.

Cost drivers without the guesswork

Prices vary by model and by the amount of work around the unit. The biggest influences are the gas line upgrade, the vent run, the location of the unit and the complexity of the existing plumbing. A straightforward swap from tank to tankless in the same spot costs less than a move to a different wall.

Cypress conditions that matter

Golden State Water reports an average hardness of 237 mg/L, or about 13.8 grains per gallon, for its West Orange County system. Readings in its 2025 data ranged from roughly 63 to 369 mg/L. Tankless heat exchangers are narrow, so scale builds up on them faster than on a tank. A yearly descaling flush is a reasonable expectation here, and a water softener upstream reduces it.

The City of Cypress Building Division requires permits for water heaters and plumbing work, with inspections Monday through Friday from 8:00 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. and about two days of notice suggested.

A permitted install is documented for your next sale, and the inspector confirms the vent and gas connections. Homeowners in La Palma and Buena Park should check their own city's rules, because permit processes are set locally.

Is tankless right for your household?

A tankless heater suits a household that wants endless hot water, has limited floor space or plans to stay in the home long enough to recoup the higher purchase price. It is a weaker fit for a tight budget or a house where gas upgrades would be extensive.

A tank can still be the better answer. See water heater installation for the standard path, and water softener and filtration if scale is your main worry.

What to expect on installation day

The crew arrives with the unit, vent parts, isolation valves and a gas line kit if one is needed. They shut off the gas and water, drain the old tank, disconnect it and carry it out. The wall area is prepared with a mounting board if required, and the new unit is hung and leveled.

The gas, water and vent connections follow, then the condensate line on condensing models. The technician fills the lines, purges air, checks every joint for leaks, lights the unit and runs hot water at several fixtures to confirm temperature and flow. You get a walk through of the display and the settings, and a note on when to flush it.

Common mistakes to avoid

The most frequent mistakes are sizing the unit by price instead of flow, skipping isolation valves that make flushing easy and reusing an undersized gas line. Another is venting through the wrong material. Each one leads to error codes, poor performance or an unsafe unit, and each is cheaper to prevent than to fix later.

After the install

Register the warranty, note the install date and schedule the first flush. Keep the isolation valve kit labeled so anyone can find it. If you see an error code, write it down before calling.

Questions to ask before you buy

  • What gas line size does this model need?
  • Where will the vent run, and what clearances apply?
  • Does the unit include isolation valves, or are they extra?
  • How often should it be flushed in hard water?
  • Who pulls the permit and schedules the inspection?
